### **Introduction**

Simon Wardley’s decade-long surveys of organisational “phenotypes” offer a useful counterpoint to the hype-driven narrative of AI takeover. His work highlights how new technologies evolve gradually, and how **practices, not hype, define the real shifts** in organisational capability. These insights integrate directly with the neo-freebooter framework.

### **1. AI as a Field, Not a Thing**

Wardley stresses that AI is not a monolith but a **field with multiple components**. Each component industrialises over time:

- From novel “genesis” experiments → to bespoke applications → to productised services → to full commodities.
- With each step, **new practices emerge**. These practices—not the technology in isolation—are what transform organisations.

This echoes our thesis that **AI collaboration literacy**, not “AI adoption,” is the dividing line for freebooter viability.

### **2. Practices Over Hype**

Wardley’s population studies identify shifts long before they become mainstream. Examples:

- 2011: **Cell-based structures, continuous deployment, commodity infrastructure.**
- 2021: **Outcome-driven work, swarming orchestration, supply chains as models, AI replacing tasks not jobs.**

Each of these sounded implausible at the time but later became accepted best practice. The lesson: **real change arrives as emergent practice, not executive hype.**

For neo-freebooters, this means value lies in adopting **emergent practices early**, not in chasing whatever “AI solution” is being sold to corporates this quarter.

### **3. The Lag in Social Systems**

Wardley observes a 20–30 year lag between cutting-edge practice and mainstream enterprise adoption:

- Social systems change slowly.
- Executives learn slowly.
- Many companies survive by **luck or relative mediocrity** (the Red Queen effect: “you only need to be as useless as the person standing next to you”).

This underscores why **freebooters, with smaller scale and higher agility, can move faster**. They can test, adopt, and normalise new practices years before corporates de-risk them.

### **4. Implications for Freebooters**

- **Avoid the hype trap**: Freebooters should not treat AI as a singular “thing” to adopt, but as a **set of evolving tools and practices** to integrate where useful.
- **Focus on outcomes, not outputs**: Embedding AI to deliver better results (outcomes) aligns with both agile practice and Burner reciprocity, avoiding the corporate obsession with metrics detached from value.
- **Experiment with emergent practices**: Swarming, cell-based teams, outcome-orientation, supply chain modelling—these are naturally suited to node-based, commons-oriented systems.
- **Leverage lag as advantage**: By adopting practices 10–20 years ahead of mainstream enterprises, freebooters position themselves not as competitors in the corporate race but as **niche innovators with resilience**.

### **5. Strategic Alignment**

Wardley’s lens supports the strategic overlay in the neo-freebooter thesis:

- **VSM System 4 (intelligence)** is strengthened by situational awareness mapping.
- **Agile trust and transparency** are reinforced by focusing on outcomes and swarming orchestration.
- **Commons literacy** ensures these practices are accessible and replicable beyond isolated actors.

### **Conclusion**

Wardley’s organisational phenotype studies validate a key neo-freebooter principle: **technology matters less than the practices it enables**. The AI hype cycle distracts corporates into posturing, while freebooters can quietly embed the next-generation practices—outcome orientation, swarming orchestration, cell-based organisation—that make resilience real. In this way, the freebooter class can outpace corporate giants not by scale, but by agility and situational awareness.
