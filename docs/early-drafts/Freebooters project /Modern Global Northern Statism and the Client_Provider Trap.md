## **Modern Global Northern Statism and the Client/Provider Trap**

### **1. The Welfare State as Provider**

Postwar welfare states in the global North were built on a social contract: the state would provide education, healthcare, pensions, housing support, and in return, citizens would contribute through work and taxation.

- **Strength**: This model lifted millions out of poverty, created stability, and underpinned economic growth.
- **Weakness**: It entrenched a **provider/client dynamic**. The state was cast as caretaker; citizens as recipients.

### **2. Resentment Through Expectations**

The provider model breeds resentment in multiple directions:

- **When provision falters**: Under austerity, economic downturn, or bureaucratic failure, citizens feel betrayed. Needs framed as entitlements go unmet, and unmet entitlements breed hostility.
- **When provision is unequal**: Groups that perceive others as receiving “more than their share” — whether migrants, welfare recipients, or different regions — channel resentment into political backlash.
- **When provision infantilises**: Citizens treated as clients rather than co-creators feel disempowered, fostering disengagement or hostility toward institutions.

### **3. Political Polarisation as a Byproduct**

The client/provider framing feeds polarisation:

- **The populist right** attacks the state as overbearing and unfair, stoking resentment at “others” supposedly favoured by the provider.
- **The liberal centre-left** defends provision but often doubles down on technocratic paternalism — emphasising competence and fairness, but not co-creation.
- **The radical left** criticises unmet promises of emancipation, pushing for more provision without changing the underlying relationship.

Across all sides, the state remains “provider,” citizens remain “clients,” and the cycle of unmet expectations persists.

### **4. Why This Matters for Neo-Freebooters**

Neo-Freebooters must avoid replicating this trap.

- If freebooter commons are perceived as **providers** (of jobs, tools, AI literacy, or resilience), they will be resented the moment their capacity falls short.
- If they appear to distribute unequally, resentment will flow in the same way it does around welfare debates.
- If they centralise too much, they risk infantilising participants, turning them into clients rather than co-creators.

### **5. Alternative: The Co-Creator Ethos**

The Burner lesson offers a counter-model:

- **Self-reliance first** — everyone is expected to bring something to the table.
- **Gifting, not entitlement** — material spillovers are framed as generosity, not provision.
- **Transparency & participation** — when trade-offs are visible and collective, criticism doesn’t calcify into betrayal.

In this frame, resilience is built not by a paternal provider state, but by **distributed co-creators**. The state — or commons institutions — act as facilitators of co-creation, not as caretakers with infinite responsibility.

⚖️ **Conclusion**:<br> Modern global northern statism illustrates the limits of the provider/client relationship. It generates resentment when promises falter, when provision is perceived as unfair, or when citizens feel infantilised. For Neo-Freebooters, the key lesson is to reject the provider role altogether: design systems where resilience flows from co-creation, self-reliance, and visible reciprocity, rather than entitlements that inevitably disappoint.
